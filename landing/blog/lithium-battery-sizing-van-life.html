Lithium Battery Sizing for Van Life: 200Ah vs 300Ah

By OffroadWatt June 29, 2026 14 min read

Choosing between a 200Ah and a 300Ah lithium battery is the single most consequential decision in a full-time van build. Too small and you spend every cloudy day rationing power. Too large and you carry dead weight that could have been solar panels or water. This guide walks through real daily consumption numbers, compares the two most popular sizes head to head, and gives you a clear framework to pick the right capacity for your lifestyle.

1. Why battery sizing matters for full-time van life

Weekend campers can get away with an undersized battery — they are back home in two days and the alternator tops up on the drive back. Full-time van life is different. Your battery bank is your only power source between charging sessions, and those sessions (solar, driving, shore power) are not always predictable.

An undersized battery forces you to constantly manage power: turning off the fridge overnight, skipping the morning coffee, driving to charge instead of exploring. An oversized battery means unnecessary weight (every kilogram matters in a van), higher upfront cost, and wasted space that could have held a water tank or storage drawer.

The sweet spot is a battery that covers 2–3 days of normal use without recharging, so you have a comfortable buffer for cloudy days, winter camps, or lazy days when you do not drive.

4. When 200Ah is enough

A 200Ah lithium battery is the right choice when:

  • Your daily draw is under 60 Ah. This covers a fridge, lights, phones, laptop, water pump and a vent fan — essentially a well-equipped van without high-draw luxuries.
  • You have reliable solar. With 200–300W of roof solar and an MPPT controller, you can replenish 40–60 Ah/day in summer. Combined with 200Ah of storage, you rarely dip below 50% state of charge.
  • You drive regularly. A 30A DC-DC charger puts back ~21 Ah per hour of driving. If you move every 1–2 days, the alternator keeps your battery near full.
  • You travel in sunny climates. Southern Europe, the US Southwest, Australia — anywhere with 4+ peak sun hours most of the year.
  • Weight matters. In a smaller van (VW T5/T6, Renault Trafic), every kilogram counts against payload capacity. A 200Ah battery saves 8–12 kg compared to 300Ah.

5. When you need 300Ah (or more)

Step up to 300Ah (or build a larger bank) when:

  • You run power-hungry appliances. An inverter driving a coffee machine, hair dryer, electric blanket or induction hob can easily push daily consumption above 80 Ah.
  • You work remotely from the van. A desktop monitor, external hard drives and 8 hours of laptop use add 15–25 Ah/day on top of basic living loads.
  • You camp in cold climates. A diesel heater running 12–16 hours/day in winter adds 15–25 Ah, and solar yield drops to 15–30% of summer levels.
  • You stay parked for 3+ days. Festival parking, remote trailheads, or national parks with no driving between sites. You need the buffer to ride out low-solar periods.
  • You want peace of mind. Some van lifers simply sleep better knowing they have a larger cushion. If the extra €300–500 and 10 kg are worth that comfort, it is a valid reason.

Never mix chemistries. Parallel batteries must be the same chemistry (all LiFePO4), same nominal voltage (all 12.8V), and ideally the same brand and model. Mismatched internal resistances cause uneven charging and reduce total lifespan.

7. Weight, space and cost

Every decision in a van build involves trade-offs. Here is how the two main sizes compare beyond raw capacity:

Weight

A 200Ah LiFePO4 weighs roughly 22–26 kg. A 300Ah unit weighs 30–38 kg. The difference (8–12 kg) is modest for a large Sprinter or Ducato, but meaningful in a T5 or Trafic where total payload might be only 600–800 kg. Remember that water (100L = 100 kg), passengers, and gear eat into payload fast.

Space

Lithium batteries are compact for their capacity. A typical 200Ah battery occupies about 27 litres. A 300Ah battery needs roughly 31 litres — only 4 litres more. The real constraint is usually height: make sure the battery fits under a seat, bench, or in a dedicated compartment with ventilation space around it.

Cost per usable Ah

At 80% DoD, a €800 / 200Ah battery gives you 160 usable Ah at €5.00 per usable Ah. A €1,200 / 300Ah battery gives you 240 usable Ah at €5.00 per usable Ah. The cost per usable Ah is very similar, so the decision is not about value — it is about whether you need the extra capacity.

8. Charging strategy and battery size

A larger battery is not always the answer. Sometimes a better charging setup solves the problem at lower cost and lower weight:

Solar

Upgrading from 200W to 400W of solar panels can add 30–50 Ah/day in summer. Two extra 100W panels cost about €150–250 and weigh 8–14 kg. That extra daily input might make a 200Ah battery sufficient where a 300Ah was needed without it.

DC-DC charger

A 30A DC-DC charger generates ~21 Ah per driving hour (70% efficiency). If you drive 1–2 hours daily, that is 21–42 Ah — enough to cover 30–50% of a typical day’s consumption. Upgrading from a 20A to a 40A B2B charger costs about €100 more and delivers 28 Ah/hour instead of 14.

Shore power

If you occasionally stay at campsites with hook-ups, a 20A mains charger can fully recharge a 200Ah battery in about 10 hours or a 300Ah in about 15 hours. One night on hook-up resets everything.

Rule of thumb: if your charging capacity (solar + alternator) can replace your daily consumption on an average day, a 200Ah battery is enough. If it cannot, either increase charging or increase storage — but increasing charging is usually cheaper and lighter.

9. The decision framework

Still unsure? Walk through these four steps:

  1. Calculate your daily Ah draw. Use the OffroadWatt calculator or the table above. Be honest — include the coffee machine and the hair dryer.
  2. Estimate your daily charging input. Solar (check your zone), alternator (amps × driving hours × 0.7), and any shore power days.
  3. Compute the deficit. On a worst-case day (cloudy, no driving), how many Ah do you consume with zero recharging?
  4. Multiply the deficit by your target buffer. Want 2 days of autonomy without any input? Multiply your daily draw by 2 and divide by 0.8 (for 80% DoD). That is your minimum battery size.

Frequently asked questions

Is 200Ah enough for full-time van life?

A 200Ah lithium battery provides 160Ah of usable capacity at 80% depth of discharge. For a moderate setup drawing 50–60Ah per day (fridge, lights, phones, water pump), 200Ah gives you roughly 2.5–3 days of autonomy without any recharging. With 200–300W of solar and occasional driving, 200Ah is enough for most full-time van lifers who are not running high-draw appliances like air conditioning, electric cooking or a large inverter for power tools.

How much does a 300Ah lithium battery cost compared to 200Ah?

In 2026, a quality 200Ah 12V LiFePO4 battery costs between €600 and €1,000 depending on brand and BMS features. A 300Ah unit typically costs €900 to €1,500. The price per Ah decreases slightly with larger capacities. Two 200Ah batteries in parallel (400Ah total) usually cost 15–25% more than a single 300Ah battery but give you more total capacity and redundancy.

Can I use two 100Ah batteries instead of one 200Ah?

Yes. Two 100Ah lithium batteries wired in parallel give you 200Ah total capacity and behave identically to a single 200Ah battery. The advantages are easier installation in tight spaces, better weight distribution, and redundancy if one battery fails. The disadvantages are more wiring, more connection points that can loosen, and slightly higher total cost than a single 200Ah unit.

How many days of autonomy do I get with 200Ah vs 300Ah?

Autonomy depends entirely on your daily consumption. At 50Ah/day (moderate use), a 200Ah lithium battery lasts about 3.2 days and a 300Ah lasts about 4.8 days without any recharging. At 80Ah/day (heavy use with diesel heater and inverter), 200Ah lasts about 2 days and 300Ah lasts about 3 days. These numbers assume 80% depth of discharge. Solar, alternator or shore power charging extends autonomy significantly.
