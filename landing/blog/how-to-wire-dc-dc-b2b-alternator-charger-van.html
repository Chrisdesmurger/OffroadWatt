Alternator charging guide

How to Wire a DC-DC (B2B) Alternator Charger in a Van

By OffroadWatt June 10, 2026 12 min read

Solar panels are great when the sun is out, but driving is the fastest way to fill a leisure battery. A DC-DC (battery-to-battery) charger takes raw alternator output and converts it into a clean, multi-stage charge tailored to your battery chemistry. Wiring one correctly is straightforward if you get three things right: sizing, cable gauge and fusing. This guide walks you through every step — from choosing the right unit to the final connection — so you can charge while you drive with confidence.

1. Why you need a DC-DC charger

Fifteen years ago, a simple split-charge relay was enough: the alternator pushed 14.4V into both batteries and everything worked. Modern vehicles have changed the game in two ways:

  • Smart alternators — Euro 6 engines use variable-voltage alternators that drop to 12.8V or lower when the starter battery is full, saving fuel. That voltage is far too low to charge a leisure battery, especially lithium.
  • Lithium batteries — LiFePO4 cells need a precise charge profile (14.2 to 14.6V absorption, then constant voltage) that a raw alternator cannot provide. Overcharging or undercharging damages the cells and can trigger a BMS shutdown.

A DC-DC charger solves both problems. It boosts low alternator voltage to the correct level and delivers a proper multi-stage charge — bulk, absorption, float — regardless of what the alternator is doing. Think of it as a translator between your engine and your leisure battery.

2. How a DC-DC charger works

A DC-DC charger is a voltage converter with a built-in charge controller. It sits between the starter battery (input) and the leisure battery (output), and it does three things:

  • Boosts or bucks voltage — if the alternator output is 12.8V, the charger boosts it to 14.4V. If it spikes to 15V, it bucks it down. The output is always the voltage your battery chemistry demands.
  • Limits current — a 30A charger draws no more than 30A, protecting both the alternator and the cabling from overload.
  • Runs a charge profile — bulk (max current until target voltage), absorption (hold voltage, taper current), float (maintenance). Exactly like a quality mains charger, but powered by the engine.

Most modern units also have a solar MPPT input, letting you combine alternator and solar charging in one box — useful for simplifying the system and saving space.

3. Sizing: which amperage to choose

The right size depends on three factors: your battery capacity, your typical driving time, and your alternator's spare capacity.

200 Ah LiFePO480 to 120 Ah30A3 to 4 hours
300+ Ah LiFePO4120 to 200 Ah40 to 50A3 to 5 hours

Rule of thumb: a DC-DC charger rated at 10 to 15% of your battery capacity in amps is a safe sweet spot. A 200 Ah bank pairs well with a 20 to 30A charger.

Watch your alternator. Most passenger vehicle alternators are rated 90 to 150A, but they also power the engine ECU, headlights, AC, and the starter battery. A 30A DC-DC charger draws roughly 35 to 40A from the alternator side (due to conversion losses). If your alternator is small (under 90A), stay at 20A to avoid overloading it, especially in hot weather when the alternator already derates.

4. Parts and tools you need

Before you start, gather everything so the install goes smoothly:

  • DC-DC charger — Victron Orion-Tr Smart, Renogy DCC series, Sterling B2B, or Redarc BCDC are the most popular. Choose one with a battery profile matching your chemistry (AGM, GEL, LiFePO4).
  • Positive cable — red, sized for the charger amperage and cable run length (see the table below). Use flexible multi-strand tinned copper for vibration resistance.
  • Negative cable — black, same gauge as the positive. Many installs share a chassis ground, but a dedicated return cable is safer and required by some charger manufacturers.
  • Fuses — one on the starter battery side (as close to the battery as possible), one on the leisure battery side. Use ANL or MIDI fuses for 30A and above.
  • Fuse holders — inline ANL/MIDI holders rated for the fuse size. Bolt-down style is more reliable than blade holders for high current.
  • Battery terminal lugs — crimped (not soldered) ring terminals sized for the cable and battery post.
  • Ignition sense wire — thin 1 mm squared wire from a switched 12V source (fuse box ACC/IGN circuit) to the charger's D+ or ignition input. This tells the charger the engine is running.
  • Cable grommet — rubber grommet where the cable passes through the bulkhead between engine bay and cabin, to prevent chafing.
  • Tools — hydraulic or ratchet crimp tool, wire stripper, heat shrink, multimeter, drill, cable ties, split loom for protection.

5. Step-by-step wiring

The wiring follows one simple path: starter battery → fuse → DC-DC charger input → DC-DC charger output → fuse → leisure battery. Here is the detailed sequence:

Step 1: Disconnect both batteries

Remove the negative terminal from the starter battery and the leisure battery. This is non-negotiable — you are about to work with high-current cables that can weld a wrench to a terminal if shorted.

Step 2: Mount the DC-DC charger

Choose a ventilated location — under a seat, on a bulkhead, or in the garage area. The charger generates heat under load (a 30A unit dissipates roughly 30 to 50W of heat). Leave at least 50 mm clearance on all sides for airflow. Mount it vertically if the manufacturer recommends it for convection cooling.

Step 3: Run the positive cable from the starter battery

Route a positive cable from the starter battery positive terminal through the bulkhead to the charger's input positive terminal. Install a fuse within 300 mm of the starter battery — this protects the entire cable run in case of a short. Use a rubber grommet and split loom where the cable passes through metal panels.

Step 4: Connect the negative (input side)

Run a negative cable from the charger's input negative terminal to the starter battery negative terminal, or to a solid chassis ground point that connects directly to the starter battery negative. A dedicated cable back to the battery is the cleanest approach and avoids ground loops.

Step 5: Connect the output to the leisure battery

Run a positive cable from the charger's output positive terminal to the leisure battery positive terminal. Install a fuse within 300 mm of the leisure battery. Connect the charger's output negative terminal to the leisure battery negative terminal (or its bus bar).

Step 6: Wire the ignition sense

Connect a thin wire (1 mm squared) from a 12V source that is only live when the ignition is on (ACC or IGN circuit in the fuse box) to the charger's D+ or ignition input. This prevents the charger from draining the starter battery when the engine is off. Some chargers detect voltage rise automatically and do not need this wire — check your manual.

Step 7: Set the battery profile

Before reconnecting, set the charger's DIP switches or app settings to your battery chemistry: AGM, GEL, or LiFePO4. Getting this wrong means the wrong charge voltage — which damages the battery over time.

Step 8: Reconnect and test

Reconnect the negative terminals on both batteries. Start the engine. The charger should detect alternator voltage and begin charging within a few seconds. Check with a multimeter that the output voltage matches the expected charge profile (14.2 to 14.6V for lithium, 14.4 to 14.8V for AGM).

Safety: never work on high-current DC wiring with the batteries connected. A short circuit on a 200 Ah battery can deliver thousands of amps instantly — enough to cause burns, fire or an explosion. Always disconnect, always fuse.

6. Cable gauge and fuse sizing table

Undersized cable is the number one cause of poor charging performance and heat buildup. Use this table to select the right gauge based on charger rating and cable run length (one-way distance from battery to charger).

Fuse sizing rule: the fuse protects the cable, not the charger. Size it to the cable's continuous current rating, which should be above the charger's maximum draw. A 30A charger on 10 mm² cable gets a 40A fuse — enough headroom to avoid nuisance trips, but low enough to protect the cable before it overheats.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

  • No fuse at the starter battery. The cable run from engine bay to cabin is the most vulnerable to chafing and shorts. Without a fuse within 300 mm of the starter battery, a short anywhere on that cable draws unlimited current from the starter battery — fire risk.
  • Undersized cable. A 4 mm² cable on a 30A charger over a 4-metre run drops so much voltage that the charger cannot boost properly. The result: slow charging and hot cables. Always use the table above.
  • Wrong battery profile. Setting AGM mode when you have lithium means the charger tops out at 14.8V and floats high — stressing lithium cells. Setting lithium mode on AGM undercharges. Double-check your DIP switches or app settings.
  • No ignition sense wire. Without it, the charger may run 24/7 and slowly drain the starter battery overnight. Even chargers with auto-detect can sometimes misjudge — wiring the D+ input is cheap insurance.
  • Poor grounding. A high-resistance ground connection (corroded chassis bolt, thin wire) causes voltage drop on the return path, reducing effective charge current and potentially confusing the charger's voltage sensing. Use a proper cable and clean the contact point to bare metal.
  • Mounting in a sealed box. DC-DC chargers derate (reduce output) above 40 degrees Celsius. Putting one in an unventilated enclosure means it throttles back on hot days — exactly when you need it most.

Frequently asked questions

What size DC-DC charger do I need for my campervan?

For most campervans with a 100 to 200 Ah lithium battery, a 20 to 30A DC-DC charger is ideal. It charges the leisure battery in 4 to 6 hours of driving without overloading the alternator. If your alternator is under 90A, stay at 20A. Larger battery banks (300 Ah or more) benefit from a 40 to 50A unit.

Can I charge a lithium battery directly from the alternator?

Not safely in most cases. Modern smart alternators vary their output voltage to save fuel, which gives an inconsistent charge that can damage lithium cells or trigger BMS shutdowns. A DC-DC charger regulates the voltage to exactly what the battery needs and protects both the alternator and the battery.

What cable gauge do I need between the alternator and the DC-DC charger?

For a 20A charger with a cable run under 3 metres, 6 mm squared cable is sufficient. For 30A or longer runs up to 5 metres, use 10 mm squared. For 40 to 50A units, go to 16 mm squared or above. Always match the fuse rating to the cable capacity, not the charger rating.

Where should I mount the DC-DC charger in the van?

Mount it in a ventilated area away from heat sources. DC-DC chargers generate heat under load and most derate their output above 40 degrees Celsius. Under a seat, in a garage area, or on a bulkhead near the battery are common spots. Keep it accessible for maintenance and away from water ingress.
