Campervan Fridge Power Consumption: Compressor vs Absorption

By OffroadWatt June 21, 2026 11 min read

The fridge is almost always the single biggest electrical consumer in a campervan — running 24 hours a day, seven days a week. Choosing between a compressor fridge and an absorption fridge has a massive impact on your battery bank, your solar needs and your off-grid autonomy. This guide gives you real-world power figures, not datasheet optimism, so you can size your electrical system with confidence.

1. How each technology works

Understanding the cooling mechanism explains why the power figures are so different.

Compressor fridges

A compressor fridge works exactly like your kitchen fridge at home. A motorised compressor circulates refrigerant through an evaporator, absorbing heat from inside the cabinet. The compressor cycles on and off — when the interior reaches the target temperature it shuts down and draws zero power. This duty cycle is the key to its efficiency: a fridge rated at 5A only draws that 5A during the roughly 30–50% of the time the compressor is actually running.

Compressor fridges run exclusively on 12V DC (or 24V). They cool faster, reach lower temperatures (down to −18°C for freezer compartments) and perform consistently regardless of ambient temperature — up to about 43°C.

Absorption fridges

Absorption fridges use a heat-driven chemical cycle — ammonia, hydrogen and water — with no moving parts. The heat source can be a gas flame (LPG), a 12V heating element or a 230V element. On gas, they are remarkably thrifty: a small flame and a tiny 12V current for the control circuit. On 12V alone, however, they are extremely power-hungry because converting electricity into heat is inherently inefficient for cooling.

Absorption fridges are common in factory-fitted motorhomes (Dometic, Thetford) and work well when you have a gas supply. They struggle in high ambient temperatures and must be parked roughly level to function properly.

3. How ambient temperature changes everything

The hotter the environment, the harder any fridge must work. Compressor fridges handle this by running longer duty cycles. Absorption fridges lose cooling capacity entirely above ~32°C.

Gas is cheapest day-to-day, but requires carrying propane bottles, regular refills and ventilation provisions. A compressor fridge on solar is effectively free to run once the panels are paid for — and many van lifers find the simplicity of an all-electric system worth the upfront investment.

7. Which fridge type for your setup?

There is no universally “better” technology. The right choice depends on your travel style, your battery bank and your climate.

Choose a compressor fridge if…

  • You want an all-electric system with no gas dependency
  • You travel in warm climates (southern Europe, tropics) where absorption units struggle
  • You have 200W+ of solar and a 200Ah+ lithium bank
  • You want consistent cooling regardless of ambient temperature or parking angle
  • You need a freezer compartment

Choose an absorption fridge if…

  • You already have a gas installation (factory-fitted motorhome)
  • You travel in temperate climates and rarely face 35°C+
  • You have a small battery bank and limited solar (the fridge runs on gas while parked)
  • You value silent operation (absorption has no compressor noise)
  • You stay on hook-ups frequently (the 230V element is efficient)

8. Tips to cut fridge power consumption

Regardless of technology, these practical habits can cut consumption by 15–30%:

  • Pre-cool food at home. Putting warm food into the fridge forces the compressor to run overtime. Load your fridge with already-cold items.
  • Shade and ventilate. Park with the fridge side in shade, or fit a ventilation fan behind a built-in unit. Every 5°C drop in ambient temperature saves roughly 10–15% consumption.
  • Keep it full. A full fridge holds temperature better than a half-empty one. Fill gaps with water bottles — they act as thermal mass.
  • Limit door openings. Every time you open the lid or door, warm air rushes in. Plan your meals and grab everything in one go.
  • Insulate the fridge. For portable compressor fridges, aftermarket insulation jackets (or a simple foam-board surround) reduce duty cycle by 10–20%.
  • Set realistic temperatures. 5°C is fine for most food storage. Dropping to 2°C increases consumption by 15–25% with minimal food-safety benefit.
  • Clean the condenser. Dust on the condenser coils or fins reduces heat dissipation. Wipe them down every few months.

Frequently asked questions

How many amps does a 12V compressor fridge use per day?

A typical 40–50 litre 12V compressor fridge draws 30 to 45 Ah per day at an ambient temperature of 25°C. The compressor cycles on and off, running roughly 30–50% of the time, so average draw is 2–4 amps rather than the peak 5–8 amps shown on the label.

Does an absorption fridge use more power than a compressor?

On 12V electricity alone, yes. An absorption fridge draws 8–12 amps continuously when running on 12V, consuming 100–150 Ah per day. On gas (LPG), however, it uses almost no electrical power — only about 200–300 grams of propane per day with negligible 12V draw for the control circuit.

Can a 100Ah battery run a compressor fridge?

A 100Ah lithium battery (80Ah usable) can power a typical 40-litre compressor fridge for roughly 2 days without any recharge. With 200W of solar generating 40–60 Ah per day in summer, the same battery could sustain the fridge indefinitely in good conditions. A 100Ah AGM battery (50Ah usable) would last about 1–1.5 days.

Is it cheaper to run a campervan fridge on gas or 12V?

Gas is cheaper per day of cooling — about €0.30–0.50 versus €0.50–0.80 for a compressor on 12V (amortised cost). However, compressor fridges on solar are effectively free to run once the panels are paid for, and many van lifers prefer them to avoid carrying gas bottles and the associated safety considerations.
