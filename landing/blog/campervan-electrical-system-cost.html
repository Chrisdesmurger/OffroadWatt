How Much Does a Campervan Electrical System Cost?

By OffroadWatt June 20, 2026 12 min read

The electrical system is one of the most important — and most confusing — parts of any campervan build. How much should you actually budget? The answer ranges from under €500 for a bare-bones weekend setup to €4,000+ for a full off-grid system with lithium, solar and a proper inverter. This guide breaks down every component, gives you real 2026 prices, and shows three complete builds at different budget levels so you can plan with confidence.

1. The 7 components you need to budget for

Every campervan electrical system, from a simple weekend rig to a full-time off-grid setup, consists of the same core building blocks. The difference between a €500 build and a €4,000 build is the quality and capacity of each block, not a fundamentally different architecture.

  • Battery bank — stores the energy you use at night and on cloudy days.
  • Solar panels — generate free energy while parked.
  • Charge controller — regulates solar input to protect the battery.
  • DC-DC charger — charges the leisure battery from the alternator while driving.
  • Inverter — converts 12V DC to 230V AC for household appliances.
  • Wiring, fuses and distribution — the nervous system that connects everything safely.
  • Monitoring and extras — battery monitor, shore power inlet, USB outlets, switches.

Below, we price each component with real 2026 products, then assemble three complete builds so you can see exactly where the money goes.

2. Battery bank: the biggest line item

The battery is typically 30–45% of your total electrical budget. The chemistry you choose — AGM, GEL or lithium (LiFePO4) — determines not just the upfront cost but also usable capacity, weight and lifespan.

Cost per usable Ah: AGM looks cheap, but you only use 50% of its rated capacity. A €150 AGM 100Ah gives you 50 usable Ah (€3.00/Ah). A €400 lithium 100Ah gives you 80 usable Ah (€5.00/Ah) — but lasts 5–8 times longer, making the cost per cycle far lower.

4. DC-DC charger (alternator charging)

A DC-DC (battery-to-battery) charger lets you top up the leisure battery while driving. Modern Euro 6 alternators use smart voltage regulation, which means a simple split-charge relay no longer works reliably — a proper DC-DC charger is now standard.

For most van builds, a 30A DC-DC charger at €130–200 is the sweet spot. At 30A you recover about 25 Ah per hour of driving — enough to meaningfully top up even a 200Ah lithium bank during a travel day.

9. Where to save — and where not to

Not every euro in the budget carries equal weight. Here is where experienced builders save money and where they refuse to cut corners:

Safe to save on

  • Solar panels. Unbranded monocrystalline panels perform nearly identically to premium brands. The cell technology is mature; pay for watts, not logos.
  • USB outlets and switches. Generic flush-mount USB-C sockets work fine. No need for Victron-branded everything.
  • Cable management accessories. Cable ties, grommets and conduit are commodity items.
  • Shore power inlet. A basic CEE inlet and MCB breaker does the job; a Victron EasyPlus is nice, not necessary.

Never save on

  • Battery quality. Cheap lithium cells with unknown BMS boards are a fire risk. Buy from established brands (Fogstar, Renogy, Victron, EVE/BYD cells with a reputable BMS).
  • Fuses and overcurrent protection. Every positive wire must be fused within 200mm of the battery terminal. Use correctly rated ANL/Mega fuses, not random blade fuses on high-current lines.
  • Cable sizing. Undersized cables cause voltage drop, heat and fire. Use a cable sizing calculator and err on the side of thicker.
  • Charge controller. A genuine MPPT controller (Victron, Renogy, EPEver) pays for itself in extra solar harvest. Avoid unbranded "MPPT" controllers that are actually PWM inside.
False economy: buying the cheapest possible battery or skipping fuses to save €50 can cost you the entire van in a fire. Electrical safety is not the place to cut costs.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a basic campervan electrical system cost?

A basic weekend-trip setup with an AGM battery, simple wiring, a PWM solar controller and a small panel costs between €400 and €700. This covers lighting, phone charging and a 12V fridge, but not high-draw 230V appliances.

Is lithium worth the extra cost in a campervan?

Yes for most builders who plan to use the van regularly. A lithium battery costs roughly twice as much as AGM upfront, but delivers 3–5 times more charge cycles, 80% usable capacity versus 50%, and weighs half as much. Over three years of regular use the cost per cycle is actually lower.

Can I install the electrical system myself?

The 12V DC side (battery, solar controller, fuses, LED lighting, USB outlets) is well within DIY reach if you follow wiring standards and fuse every circuit. The 230V AC side (inverter output, mains hookup) should be inspected or installed by a qualified electrician in most countries.

What is the most expensive part of a campervan electrical system?

The battery bank is almost always the single biggest line item, typically 30–45% of the total budget. A quality 200Ah lithium battery alone costs €600–900. Solar panels are comparatively cheap per watt; the controller, inverter and wiring add up to roughly the same as the battery.
