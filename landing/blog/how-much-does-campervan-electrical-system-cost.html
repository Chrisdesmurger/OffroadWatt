How Much Does a Campervan Electrical System Cost? Full Budget Breakdown

By OffroadWatt June 20, 2026 12 min read

The electrical system is the backbone of any self-contained campervan — and one of the most confusing line items to budget for. Prices vary wildly depending on battery chemistry, solar capacity and whether you install it yourself. This guide breaks the cost into seven clear categories, gives you real 2026 component prices, and presents three complete builds at different budgets so you know exactly what to expect.

1. Cost overview: what goes into a campervan electrical system

A complete campervan electrical system is made up of seven cost categories. Understanding each one stops you from overspending on one area and under-budgeting another.

For a mid-range DIY build in 2026, total materials cost falls between €1,500 and €2,500. Professional installation adds €500–1,500 on top. We will price every component in the sections below.

2. Batteries: the biggest single expense

The battery bank stores all the energy you use when parked. Its cost depends on three things: chemistry, capacity and brand.

Cost per usable Ah over the battery’s lifetime: a 100Ah AGM at €150 and 500 cycles delivers 25,000 usable Ah total — €0.006/Ah. A 200Ah LiFePO4 at €800 and 3,000 cycles delivers 480,000 usable Ah — €0.0017/Ah. Lithium costs 3–4× less per Ah over its life.

For weekend trips, a single 100Ah AGM (€150) is enough. For full-time van life, most builders choose a 200–300Ah LiFePO4 bank (€600–1,500) because the long-term cost per amp-hour is dramatically lower and the weight saving is significant.

A typical mid-range setup is 2×200W rigid panels + MPPT 100/30, costing around €400–580 installed on the roof. Budget builds can start with a single 100W panel and a PWM controller for under €100.

Flexible panels look sleek but cost 50–80% more per watt, have shorter lifespans (5–8 years vs 20+ years for rigid), and run hotter, which reduces output. Choose them only if roof clearance is critical.

4. DC-DC charger (alternator charging)

A DC-DC charger (also called a B2B or battery-to-battery charger) charges your leisure battery from the vehicle’s alternator while you drive. It is the most reliable charging source because it works rain or shine.

ChargerRatingPrice range
Victron Orion-Tr Smart 12/12-3030A€180–220

Victron Orion XS 12/12-5050A€280–350

Most campervans use a 30A unit (€180–220), which adds about 21Ah per hour of driving (30A × 0.7 efficiency). For heavy users or 24V systems, a 50A charger makes sense despite the higher cost.

Combo units: the Renogy DCC50S combines a 50A DC-DC charger with a 50A MPPT solar controller in one box for around €240. It can save you €100–150 compared to buying both separately — but you lose the modularity to upgrade each independently.

5. Inverter (12V to 230V)

An inverter converts your 12V battery power to 230V AC so you can run mains appliances like a laptop charger, coffee machine or hair dryer. Not every build needs one — if all your devices run on 12V or USB, you can skip it and save €100–400.

InverterTypeRatingPrice range

A pure sine wave inverter is essential for sensitive electronics (laptops, CPAP machines). Modified sine wave inverters are cheaper but can damage or malfunction with many devices. For most campervans, a 500–1,200W pure sine inverter (€130–380) covers all realistic needs.

The “invisible” cost that catches many builders off guard. Cables, fuses, bus bars, terminal lugs and a distribution panel add up quickly.

Total wiring cost for a typical mid-range build: €150–300. Budget builds can get away with €80–120 by using shorter cable runs and fewer circuits. Do not skimp on cable cross-section — undersized cables cause voltage drop, heat and fire risk.

Safety first: every positive cable must be fused within 200mm of the battery terminal. Use a battery isolator switch on the main positive cable so you can cut all power in an emergency. This is not optional — it is basic electrical safety.

9. Where to save — and where not to

Not every euro spent on your electrical system delivers equal value. Here is where to cut — and where cutting costs you more in the long run.

Good places to save

  • DIY installation. The single biggest savings. Professional install adds €500–1,500, but 12V wiring is safe and learnable in a weekend.
  • Skip the inverter if you can live with 12V and USB. Many USB-C PD chargers now handle laptops, phones and cameras.
  • Buy panels directly from solar wholesalers or Chinese brands (Renogy, Eco-Worthy). Branded van-specific panels are often the same cells in fancier packaging at double the price.
  • Start small and expand later. A single 100Ah lithium battery and 200W of solar is enough to prove the concept. You can always add a second battery in parallel later.

Bad places to save

  • Cheap cables. Undersized or CCA (copper-clad aluminium) cables cause voltage drop, heat build-up and fire risk. Always use pure copper, correctly sized.
  • No-name lithium batteries without a proper BMS. A faulty BMS can mean no low-temperature protection, no cell balancing and a thermal runaway risk. Stick to established brands (Victron, Renogy, Fogstar, LiTime) or DIY with reputable cells (EVE, CATL).
  • Skipping fuses. Every positive cable needs appropriate fusing. A missing fuse turns a short circuit into a fire.
  • Modified sine wave inverters for sensitive electronics. They cost half the price but can damage laptops, CPAP machines and battery chargers. The €50 saving is not worth the risk.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a basic campervan electrical system cost?

A basic weekend-trip setup with a 100Ah AGM battery, simple wiring, a manual battery switch and LED lighting costs between €400 and €700. This covers lights, phone charging and a 12V fridge, but has no solar or inverter.

Is lithium worth the extra cost for a campervan?

Yes, for most builds. A 200Ah LiFePO4 battery costs roughly 2–3 times more than an equivalent AGM setup, but delivers 2–3 times more usable cycles. Over a 5-year period, the cost per usable amp-hour is lower with lithium, and you save weight and space.

What is the most expensive part of a campervan electrical system?

The battery bank is almost always the single biggest expense, typically 30–50% of the total electrical budget. A quality 200Ah lithium battery alone costs €600–1,200. Solar panels and the MPPT controller together form the second largest cost block.

Can I install a campervan electrical system myself to save money?

Absolutely. DIY installation saves €500–1,500 in labour costs. The components are low-voltage (12V or 24V) and safe to handle with basic tools. The main skills you need are crimping terminals, routing cables and reading a wiring diagram. Many builders complete their first system in a weekend.
